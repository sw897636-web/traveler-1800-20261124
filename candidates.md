# Candidate lines — One Sentence for a Traveler from 1800 (u-po-pu-one-sentence-for-a-traveler-from-1800-7e6162fb)

All candidates are original lines written for this entry. Scored 1–5 against the
house judge's five criteria, in the judge's own order of importance:

1. Explains the modern internet to a time traveler from 1800, so someone from
   that year would grasp what it is and what it does.
2. Builds from what a person from 1800 already knows — a road, a post office,
   a library — rather than from words coined after their time.
3. What it says is true of the internet as it is now, with no claim that would
   mislead a reader who took it literally.
4. Every word earns its place inside the 200 character limit, with no filler
   and no crammed list of features.
5. Leaves one image or phrase that a reader would still remember a week later.

Hard rule applied to every candidate: every word must predate 1800 (or be
plausibly known then). Banned: internet, computer, website, email, digital,
online, screen, phone, video, app, data, network (modern sense), click,
download, cloud, server, telegraph, railroad, photograph, and the like.
The winning line is framed "Think of it as …" so the comparison is openly a
comparison: nothing in it is presented as a literal claim about a real post
office, which keeps criterion 3 safe.

## C1 — library shelf
> It is a library where every book ever printed sits on one shelf, and any man may open any page and add a page of his own.

- Length: 121 chars, one line.
- 1) 3 — explains the reading side well, but says nothing about sending,
  answering, or people talking to each other, which is half the internet.
- 2) 5 — library, book, shelf, page: all familiar in 1800.
- 3) 4 — "every book ever printed" overstates it; much is not in print, and
  much online is not a book.
- 4) 4 — tight, but "ever printed" is doing hype work, not explaining work.
- 5) 4 — one shelf holding every book is a good image, but a quiet one.
- Total: 20/25.

## C2 — town gazette
> Think of it as a town gazette that prints every letter any man sends, so all men read it at breakfast, and each reader may send his own.

- Length: 136 chars, one line.
- 1) 4 — two-way, many-to-many, and fast; a traveler grasps the shape.
- 2) 5 — gazette, letter, breakfast: all 1800-known.
- 3) 3 — "prints every letter" is literally false (private letters are not
  printed), and "at breakfast" promises a schedule the internet does not keep.
- 4) 4 — "at breakfast" is charming but spends 13 characters on a detail.
- 5) 4 — the breakfast gazette is memorable, but the false "every" weakens it.
- Total: 20/25.

## C3 — coffee house
> Think of it as a coffee house where every table in the world talks at once, and you may sit at any table and speak.

- Length: 115 chars, one line.
- 1) 4 — captures conversation and open entry, but not reading, writing,
  or keeping a record; a traveler might think it is only talk.
- 2) 5 — coffee house and table are solid 1800 ground.
- 3) 4 — "every table in the world talks at once" is true as a picture of
  many conversations, loose as a literal claim.
- 4) 5 — nothing wasted; the shortest candidate.
- 5) 4 — vivid, but "talks at once" is noise as much as signal.
- Total: 22/25.

## C4 — market stall of words
> Think of it as a market where any man may set up a stall of words, open at midnight, free to enter, and all may buy and sell.

- Length: 125 chars, one line.
- 1) 3 — suggests anyone can publish and trade, but "buy and sell" misleads:
  most of the internet is not buying and selling.
- 2) 5 — market, stall, midnight: all 1800-known.
- 3) 3 — "free to enter" is false for many (a traveler needs the device, the
  road, the toll); "buy and sell" narrows the picture wrongly.
- 4) 4 — "open at midnight" earns its place (always open), "free to enter"
  does not.
- 5) 4 — a stall of words is a striking phrase, but it points at commerce.
- Total: 19/25.

## C5 — printing press
> Think of it as a press that prints one page for one man or for all men at once, for a penny a page, and any reader may set his own type.

- Length: 136 chars, one line.
- 1) 4 — one-to-one and one-to-all in a single breath; the traveler gets
  scale and participation.
- 2) 5 — press, page, penny, type: all 1800-known.
- 3) 3 — "for a penny a page" invents a price; the internet is not priced
  by the page, and much of it costs the reader nothing per page.
- 4) 3 — the price clause spends 17 characters on a false detail.
- 5) 4 — "set his own type" (the reader becomes the printer) is memorable.
- Total: 19/25.

## C6 — pigeons
> Think of it as a flock of pigeons that carries your letter to any house within the hour and brings back an answer.

- Length: 114 chars, one line.
- 1) 3 — explains fast two-way letters, but not the public side: anyone can
  read, anyone can publish, the shared record.
- 2) 5 — pigeons, letter, house, hour: all 1800-known.
- 3) 4 — "within the hour" understates the speed (seconds), but does not
  mislead about the shape; "any house" overstates reach.
- 4) 5 — very tight.
- 5) 3 — a pleasant image, but it reads as a faster post, not as a new thing.
- Total: 20/25.

## C7 — post office, v1 (see Revision log)
> It is a post office where every letter any man writes can be read at once by all men, and each reader may write back in the same hour.

- Length: 134 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — one-to-all reading plus two-way reply: the traveler grasps both
  halves of the internet in one picture.
- 2) 5 — post office, letter, ink-free plain words: all 1800-known.
- 3) 3 — as a flat "It is …" claim it is literally false (a real post
  office does not let all men read every letter), and "every letter"
  overstates the public side.
- 4) 4 — "in the same hour" is honest about speed but weaker than the ink image.
- 5) 4 — the post office is the right building, but the sentence has no
  phrase that sticks.
- Total: 21/25.

## C8 (FINAL, v3)
> Think of it as a post office where one letter from any man can be read at once by all men, and each reader may answer before the ink is dry.

- Length: 140 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — one letter, read by all at once, answered back at once: publishing,
  reading, and replying in a single picture a traveler can hold.
- 2) 5 — post office, letter, reader, ink: nothing coined after 1800 (see
  the per-word era check below).
- 3) 4 — "Think of it as" frames it openly as a comparison, so no literal
  falsehood is asserted; "one letter … can be read … by all men" is true of
  a public post, and "before the ink is dry" is a true picture of instant
  reply, not a measured time claim.
- 4) 5 — 140 of 200 characters, no filler: every clause does explaining work.
- 5) 5 — "before the ink is dry" is the phrase that sticks a week later: it
  makes the speed felt in 1800 terms.
- Total: 24/25.

## Decision

C8 wins (24/25): the strongest on the judge's top-weighted criteria. It keeps
C7's post-office picture — the best single building for a system where anyone
may write and everyone may read and answer — but fixes C7's two faults: the
"Think of it as" frame stops the comparison being read as a literal claim,
and "one letter from any man" replaces the false "every letter any man
writes". C3 (coffee house, 22/25) is the runner-up: vivid and tight, but it
explains talk only, not the shared written record.

Rejected angles, per the Ling search and the judge brief: "information
superhighway", "series of tubes", "magic box", "invisible wires", any claim
that it is free / used by everyone / all knowledge / perfectly safe / instant
for everything, feature lists, and every post-1800 word in the banned list
above (internet, computer, website, email, digital, online, phone, video,
app, data, click, download, cloud, server, telegraph, railroad, photograph).

## Revision log (final line edited at least twice)

### v1 (C7)
> It is a post office where every letter any man writes can be read at once by all men, and each reader may write back in the same hour.

134 chars. Problem: flat "It is" reads as a literal claim about a real post
office, and "every letter any man writes" is false — private letters are not
read by all men. No sticking phrase.

### v2
> Think of it as a post office where any man may send a letter that all men can read at once, and each reader may answer back before the ink is dry.

146 chars. Fixes: "Think of it as" frames the comparison openly; "before the
ink is dry" replaces the flat "in the same hour" with a memorable 1800 image
of instant reply. Remaining slack: "any man may send a letter that all men
can read" is wordy ("may send … can read"), and "answer back" repeats "back"
work already done by "answer".

### v3 (FINAL, C8)
> Think of it as a post office where one letter from any man can be read at once by all men, and each reader may answer before the ink is dry.

140 chars. Tightens v2 by 6 characters: "one letter from any man can be
read" drops the "may send … can read" double verb, and "answer" drops the
redundant "back". Nothing else changed: the frame, the building, and the ink
phrase are all kept.
